What Is a Slip-On Flange?

A slip-on flange is bored a little larger than the pipe outside diameter so the pipe can be pushed into it, and is then fillet welded in two places — inside the bore at the pipe end, and on the back of the flange where the pipe leaves it. Those two welds are what hold the joint and keep it tight. Nothing has to be bevelled and nothing has to be cut to an exact length, which is why a fitter can set a slip-on joint far quicker than a butt-welded one.

The trade uses several names for the same family. A plain drilled disc with no hub is a plate flange or flat flange. Put an integral boss around the bore and it becomes a hub flange. Machine a sealing face inside the bolt circle and it is a slip-on raised face, or SORF; leave the face flat across the full width and it is SOFF. All of them bolt to the same drilling, so they can be mixed along one line as long as the facings and gaskets match.

Where a slip-on stops being the right answer is high pressure and repeated movement. The load path runs through two fillet welds and concentrates at the bore, so on a line that cycles in temperature, vibrates, or has to be radiographed, a weld neck flange is the correct call. For steady water, cooling, fire and utility duty the slip-on does the same job for less money.

Pick a slip-on flange when

  • The line carries water, cooling water, fire water, chilled water, drainage or plant utilities
  • Pressure is low to moderate and the service is steady rather than cyclic
  • Site alignment is awkward and pipe lengths cannot be held to the millimetre
  • You want the flange bore cut to suit the pipe already on the ground
  • Cost per joint matters and the line is not critical high-pressure service

Slip-On Flange Sub-Categories We Make

Five slip-on sub-types come off our machines: slip-on raised face (SORF), slip-on flat face (SOFF), hub slip-on, flat plate flange and reducing slip-on. For a given size and table they all share the same bolt pattern, so the choice comes down to the face you have to seal against, how much metal you want behind the weld, and whether the pipe entering the flange is the same size as the line.

Slip-On Raised Face – SORF

The everyday slip-on. A raised sealing face sits inside the bolt circle and carries a ring gasket, so the bolt load is concentrated on a narrow band and the joint holds tighter than a flat face. Specify SORF whenever the flange it bolts to is also raised face.

Slip-On Flat Face – SOFF

The whole face is flat, so a full-face gasket runs right across it and over the bolt holes. Use SOFF against pump casings, valve bodies and cast iron flanges that are flat faced themselves — a raised face bolted to a casting can crack it.

Hub Slip-On Flange

A slip-on with an integral hub raised around the bore. The extra metal sits exactly where the outer fillet weld lands, so the joint is stiffer than a plain plate flange of the same face thickness. Common once bores get large or the line takes some bending.

Flat Plate Flange

A plain drilled disc with no hub at all, cut and faced from plate. The simplest flange we make and the usual choice for tank nozzles, ducting, low-pressure water lines, and as a loose backing flange behind a stub end.

Reducing Slip-On Flange

Drilled for the line size but bored for a smaller pipe, so you can step down at a pump, valve or header without adding a reducer and a second joint. Give us both sizes — the flange thickness follows the larger one.

To Your Own Drawing

Send a sketch, a PDF or an old flange and we will match it: a non-standard pitch circle, extra tapped holes, oversize thickness, a bore for imported pipe or a facing groove. Odd jobs are normal work here.

The bore is cut to your pipe

Slip-on bores are not one fixed figure across the trade — they follow the outside diameter of the pipe going into them, which varies with the standard the pipe was made to. Where Slip-On Flanges Are Used — and Where We Send Them

Slip-on, hub and plate flanges are the working flange of water, air, chilled-water, fire-water, effluent and general utility lines — anywhere the pressure is moderate, the line is not thermally cycled hard, and a fitter needs to be able to swing the flange round on the pipe to line the bolt holes up on site. Below are the jobs our slip-on flanges leave the shop for most often, in mild steel and in stainless.

Water Supply & Pumping

Municipal mains, building risers, overhead and underground tank connections, pump suction and delivery headers. Table E mild steel SORF is the standard call; SS 304 where the water is treated or softened.

HVAC & Chilled Water

Chiller and AHU connections, chilled and condenser water headers, cooling tower piping and thermal storage tanks. Flat face flanges with full-face gaskets are common on the cast-iron pump and valve bodies in these lines.

Fire-Fighting & Sprinkler Mains

Hydrant ring mains, sprinkler headers, jockey and main pump connections. Usually Table E or Table F drilling with raised face; we also drill to the consultant's own bolt pattern where the specification calls for it.

Sewage, Effluent & ETP

Sewage rising mains, pumping station manifolds, ETP and STP transfer lines, sludge headers. SS 316 slip-on flanges where the effluent is chloride-bearing and mild steel will not last.

Irrigation & Canal Works

Distribution networks, canal outlet structures, lift irrigation headers and farm supply lines. Large repeat quantities in 100mm to 400mm NB, where a plate flange is often the cheapest way to close or branch a line.

Plant Utilities & Ship Repair

Compressed air, service water, cooling water, ballast and bilge lines, deck piping and general fabrication work. Darukhana is a ship-repair belt, so short-notice one-off and odd-size flanges are routine for us.

Slip-On, Hub and Plate Flanges — Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers to what buyers, fabricators and project engineers ask us most often about slip-on flanges. If your question is not here, call +91 99220 47887 and speak to the shop directly.

What is a slip-on flange?

+

A slip-on flange has a bore slightly larger than the pipe outside diameter, so the pipe slides into the flange before welding. It is then fillet welded twice — once inside the bore at the pipe end and once behind the flange — and those two welds carry the load and seal the joint. Because there is no machined neck and no bevel to prepare, a slip-on flange is quicker to fit and costs less than a weld neck of the same size and drilling, and it forgives small errors in pipe length. I.S. Engineering Co. machines slip-on flanges at Darukhana, Mumbai in mild steel and stainless steel from 15mm to 600mm NB.

Which slip-on flange sub-types does I.S. Engineering Co. make?

+

We make five slip-on sub-types: slip-on raised face (SORF), slip-on flat face (SOFF), hub slip-on, flat plate flange and reducing slip-on. SORF is the default for process and utility lines that use a ring gasket. SOFF is used where the mating face is flat and a full-face gasket is required. The hub type adds an integral boss around the bore for extra weld metal. The plate type is a plain drilled disc with no hub. The reducing type carries the bore of a smaller pipe with the drilling of the larger line. Any of them can be cut to your own drawing.

What is a hub flange and how is it different from a plain slip-on flange?

+

A hub flange is a slip-on flange with an integral raised hub around the bore. The hub puts extra metal exactly where the pipe meets the flange, so the outer fillet weld sits on a thicker section and the joint is stiffer than a plain plate flange of the same face thickness. Trade practice in India is to call the plain drilled disc a plate or flat flange and the hubbed version a hub flange; both bolt to the same drilling. We machine hub flanges from thicker plate or from forgings, depending on the size and grade you need.

What is the difference between a plate flange and a slip-on flange?

+

A plate flange is a flat drilled disc with no hub, so the full flange thickness does the work and the pipe is welded straight to it. A slip-on flange is bored to slide over the pipe and is welded on both sides of that bore. Plate flanges are the cheapest option and are common on tank nozzles, ducting, low-pressure water lines and as loose backing flanges. Slip-on flanges are the better choice once the line has to hold pressure or take some bending.

Slip-on or weld neck — which one should I specify?

+

Specify slip-on where the line is low to moderate pressure, the service is steady rather than cyclic, and fitting speed and cost matter — utility and cooling water, fire mains, HVAC chilled water, drainage and general plant piping. Specify weld neck where the joint has to take high pressure, thermal cycling, vibration or fatigue, or where a radiographed full-penetration weld is called for. A slip-on relies on two fillet welds and concentrates stress at the bore, which is why it is not used on critical high-pressure duty.

Which gasket suits a flat face slip-on flange?

+

A flat face flange takes a full-face gasket, one that covers the whole face including the bolt holes, so the bolt load is spread across the full face instead of bending the flange inwards. A raised face flange takes a ring gasket that sits inside the bolt circle. Do not put a ring gasket on a flat face joint, and do not bolt a raised face flange against a cast iron flat face flange, because the raised face can crack the casting.
